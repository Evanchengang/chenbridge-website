PPWR Is Now Enforceable: A Compliance Checklist for Buyers Sourcing Packaging from China

ChenBridge Insights · 7 min read

Since 12 August 2026, the EU Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation — Regulation (EU) 2025/40, known as PPWR — is generally applicable across all 27 EU member states. It replaces the 30-year-old Packaging Directive and applies directly, with no need for individual countries to transpose it. If you place packaging on the EU market, including packaging sourced from China, the grace period is over.

For buyers, PPWR turns into something very concrete: a list of documents and data you must collect from every supplier before you award a contract. Here is what the regulation requires right now, and the checklist to run with your Chinese packaging suppliers.

What PPWR Requires Right Now

The regulation entered into force on 11 February 2025 and became enforceable on 12 August 2026. From that date, every packaging type placed on the EU market needs a valid Declaration of Conformity (DoC) — a legally binding self-declaration issued by the manufacturer under the sole responsibility of the manufacturer, following the model in Annex VIII of the regulation and backed by technical documentation per Annex VII.

Alongside the DoC, substance limits apply immediately. For food-contact packaging: no more than 25 parts per billion of any individual PFAS, no more than 250 ppb for the sum of targeted PFAS, and no more than 50 ppm total fluorine. There is no transitional period for these limits. For all packaging: the combined concentration of lead, cadmium, mercury, and hexavalent chromium must not exceed 100 ppm.

Extended Producer Responsibility is being operationalised across member states, meaning someone in your supply chain must be registered as the producer in each EU country where the packaging ends up — and EPR fees are being eco-modulated, so hard-to-recycle packaging will cost more in fees. The regulation also caps empty space in e-commerce packaging at 50%.

One note for UK buyers: PPWR applies to the EU. The UK has its own packaging regime, so check both if you sell into both markets.

The Declaration of Conformity: The Document That Decides Market Access

The DoC is the single most important new deliverable in packaging sourcing. It is not a marketing certificate and not a third-party audit report. It is the manufacturer's own legally binding statement that a specific packaging type conforms to the applicable PPWR requirements — covering design, composition, recyclability, and restricted substances.

Three things buyers often get wrong about it:

It is per packaging type, not per company. A supplier's general "we are PPWR compliant" statement covers nothing. You need a DoC for every packaging type you buy from them — each material, structure, and variant.

It must match the goods. The DoC is issued against a defined specification: materials, coatings, inks, construction. If the factory changes any of these in mass production, the document no longer describes what you are importing.

It must be kept on file and kept current. When the specification changes, the DoC and the underlying technical documentation need updating. A DoC from last year's spec does not cover this year's production.

The blunt version used by procurement teams this year: no DoC on file, no market access. Make it a go/no-go item in supplier qualification.

The Checklist: What to Ask Your Chinese Supplier Now

Run through these seven items with every packaging supplier serving your EU business. Get the answers in writing, before production — not after the goods have shipped.

1. A Declaration of Conformity for every packaging type. Following the Annex VIII model, issued under the manufacturer's sole responsibility, with the technical documentation (Annex VII) available on request. Not a company-level certificate — one per packaging type and variant.

2. PFAS evidence for food-contact packaging. Ask what coatings and barrier treatments are used, then ask for test data showing compliance with the 25 ppb / 250 ppb / 50 ppm limits. Many barrier-coated boards were bought for years on performance specs alone — the converter may never have asked what is actually in the coating. Now you must.

3. Heavy metals declaration. Written confirmation that the sum of lead, cadmium, mercury, and hexavalent chromium does not exceed 100 ppm, backed by test data where appropriate.

4. Full material and composition data. The DoC is only as credible as the technical documentation behind it. Ask for the composition breakdown that supports the declaration — materials, coatings, inks, adhesives.

5. EPR responsibility, assigned in writing. Clarify who is the producer of record in each EU member state where your packaging is placed on the market, and confirm registration. Do not assume your supplier, your forwarder, or your EU entity has it covered — get it named.

6. Specification discipline for the empty-space rule. With empty space in e-commerce packaging capped at 50%, right-sizing is now a compliance question as well as a freight question. Lock dimensions against the approved spec and check them in production.

7. A pre-shipment check against the DoC'd specification. Materials get substituted, coatings get changed, grammages drift. A final check that the produced goods match the documented specification is what keeps the DoC truthful at the moment of import.

What's Coming Next: 2027–2038

PPWR is phased, and the 2026 obligations are only the first wave. What is already on the calendar:

— From 2027 onwards: digital labelling elements, including QR codes carrying packaging information, phasing in by category.
— August 2028: harmonised sorting labels across the EU, so consumers sort packaging the same way in every member state.
— 2030: design-for-recycling criteria with recyclability grades, minimum recycled-plastic content thresholds, packaging minimisation requirements, and bans on certain single-use formats.
— 2038: only packaging graded A or B for recyclability may be placed on the market.

Packaging specifications take 12 to 18 months to work through a supply chain. The specifications you lock in with suppliers today will live under the 2028 and 2030 rules — design for where the regulation is going, not just where it is.

The Part Paperwork Can't Cover

Here is the uncomfortable truth about the DoC: it is a document about a specification, and specifications live or die on the factory floor. A supplier can hold a perfectly drafted Declaration of Conformity while producing goods that no longer match it — a substituted coating here, a lighter grammage there, an ink change nobody recorded.

That gap between the documented spec and the produced goods is where most compliance risk actually sits. Closing it is not a paperwork exercise. It takes specification alignment with the supplier before production, visibility during production so deviations surface early, and a check against the approved specification before shipment.

That is the work we do locally in China: making sure the product that ships is the product the paperwork describes. If you are sourcing packaging from China for the EU market and want a second pair of eyes on your suppliers' PPWR readiness, that is a conversation worth having.

Practical Takeaway

PPWR has moved compliance from the legal department to the purchase order. The buyers who are coping well share one habit: they treat the DoC and its supporting data as deliverables with the same seriousness as price and lead time — requested at RFQ stage, verified before production, and re-checked before shipment.

Start with the seven items above. And remember that a regulation with a 2038 horizon rewards suppliers whose specifications are built to evolve, not just to pass today's check.
