Burger Box Production in China: A 4-Container Case Study

ChenBridge Cases · 4 min read

At a glance

  • Client: Leading European food brand, name withheld
  • Product: Printed folding burger boxes for food-service use
  • Order volume: 4 containers
  • ChenBridge scope: Supplier development, production coordination, in-process checks, pre-shipment inspection

The order

A leading European food brand placed a 4-container order for printed folding burger boxes in China.

On paper, the product looks simple: a printed paperboard take-out box.

In production, however, several details determine whether the order succeeds: grease resistance, food-contact material control, board consistency, print quality, and structural accuracy.

Below are the five checkpoints that mattered most on this order, and how ChenBridge helped turn the buyer's requirements into practical controls on the factory floor.

1. Grease resistance: matching the KIT rating to the end use

Burger boxes are used for medium-grease foods, so grease resistance has to be specified correctly.

Industry guidance typically places burger and sandwich packaging in the KIT 4–7 range on the TAPPI T559 grease-resistance scale, where higher numbers indicate stronger grease resistance.

If the barrier is under-specified, oil can soak through before the customer finishes the meal. If it is over-specified, the buyer may pay for performance the product does not need.

What ChenBridge did

We made sure the supplier understood that this was not dry-bakery packaging. The grease-resistance requirement was tied to the actual end use — burgers — and confirmed before production, so the right board and barrier system were used from the first production run.

2. Food-contact safety: board, inks, and coatings

For food-service packaging, every material layer matters.

The board, inks, and coatings need to be suitable for food-contact use and aligned with the destination market’s requirements, such as EU food-contact rules for Europe or relevant FDA requirements for the US market.

This includes avoiding unsuitable recycled stock where mineral-oil contamination may be a concern, and ensuring that ink and coating systems are appropriate for the application.

What ChenBridge did

Before the order moved into production, we reviewed the supplier’s food-contact documentation for the board, ink, and coating systems. This helped ensure that the factory was not making material decisions based on assumptions.

3. Board consistency: grade, grammage, and lot control

A burger box depends on the right paperboard grade and grammage for its size and use.

Just as important, the board needs to stay consistent throughout the order. A mid-run board substitution is one of the quietest ways a packaging order can go wrong.

What ChenBridge did

We wrote the board requirements clearly into the order specification and worked with the factory on incoming-material checks. Purchasing and production were aligned on the approved board, and any lot change had to be flagged instead of silently absorbed into the run.

4. Print quality: color, scuff resistance, and brand consistency

For branded food packaging, print quality is not cosmetic.

Color needs to stay consistent against the approved proof. Stacked boxes should not scuff each other in a way that damages the brand presentation. Inks also need to remain suitable for the intended food-service application.

What ChenBridge did

We translated the brand artwork into concrete production targets: color matching against the approved proof, scuff resistance expectations, and consistent print quality across the run. Print checks were carried out during production, so the standard remained active on the factory floor.

5. Structure: crease, fold, glue, and dimensions

A burger box that does not erect cleanly, close properly, or hold its shape is a failed box, even if the print looks good.

Crease quality, fold accuracy, glue adhesion, and dimensional tolerance all affect how the finished box performs in real use.

What ChenBridge did

We agreed die-cutting and dimensional tolerances with the factory before production. During the run and again before shipment, we checked construction, glue performance, folding behavior, and finished dimensions against the agreed specification.

Why this case matters

At this order volume, the biggest risk is rarely the design itself.

The real question is whether box number 100,000 matches box number 10.

That only happens when the right checkpoints are defined early, understood by the supplier, monitored during production, and verified before shipment.

This is where ChenBridge creates value: turning buyer requirements into factory-level execution.

Frequently asked questions

What are the key quality checkpoints for burger boxes?

The main checkpoints are grease resistance, food-contact safety of board, inks and coatings, board grade consistency, print quality, and structural accuracy, including crease, fold, glue, and dimensions.

How does ChenBridge help suppliers meet these checkpoints?

ChenBridge turns buyer requirements into practical shop-floor controls. This includes reviewing supplier documentation, clarifying specifications before production, monitoring key checkpoints during the run, and checking finished goods before shipment.
